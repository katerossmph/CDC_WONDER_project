Project Overview

This project analyzes trends in mortality from major chronic diseases (heart disease, diabetes, and stroke) in Massachusetts from 2021 to 2023. Analyses explore patterns by year, sex, age, group, and county, highlighting disparities and trends in deaths.

Chronic diseases such as heart disease, diabetes, and stroke are leading causes of mortality in Massachusetts. Understanding patterns across age, sex, and county can help inform public health interventions and resource allocation.

Objectives

This analysis aims to answer the following questions by:

  1. Comparing mortality by cause, sex, age group, and Massachusetts Counties

  2. Identifying trends from 2021 - 2023

  3. Highlighting high-risk populations

Discussion

Key Insights and Explanations of Patterns:

  • Heart Disease is a leading cause of death in Massachusetts

  • Middlesex, Essex, Worcester, and Norfolk County have the highest total deaths over the three years. This is interesting because these are surrounding the largest cities in Massachusetts (Boston and Worcester). The Counties immediately surrounding Boston are Suffolk County (where Boston is located), Middlesex, Essex, and Norfolk. This is intriguing because Middlesex County reported a 320.58% higher total death counts of heart disease in 2021-2023.

Conclusion

Overall, this quick retrospective research of mortality rates provides a high-level overview of selected causes of mortality in Massachusetts. It is recommended to do a further analysis investigating ALL potential causes of death recorded in the CDC WONDER database. When cross-referencing and doing a preliminary search of mortality in Massachusetts from 2017 to 2021 using the Massachusetts Department of Public Health website, it suggests that the top 7 causes of death were cancer, heart disease, COVID-19, injuries, respiratory diseases, stroke, and diabetes. It is recommended to investigate these patterns further with more expansive database abilities. It would also be good to evaluate the impacts of additional variables such as race, ethnicity, socioeconomic status, access to healthcare, education, etc.

It could also be interesting to run this analysis again and investigate relative to other states nationally, or even just regionally (in New England).

Limitations

There were some considerable limitations of this retrospective analysis.

  • Short Time Frame (2021 - 2023): This research pulled data from the “Underlying Cause of Death, 2018-2023, Single Race Request” from CDC WONDER Mortality database. Filtered specifically for data from Massachusetts, the imported data set only included information for 2021, 2022, and 2023. The short time frame is not enough to draw specific conclusions from.

  • Missing Variables: This research originally aimed to evaluate a potential causal relationship of race and how it may impact mortality rates. Due to how the CDC WONDER database is structured, I was only able to pull five specific variables to begin conducting analysis on. As such, this analysis is very much a high-level descriptive epidemiological/demographic type of analysis. It is able to indicate specific patterns based on the population but is unable to draw many conclusions about causal relationships. Next rounds of research, it would be good to investigate the relationship with additional variables such as race, ethnicity, socioeconomic data, etc.

  • Underreporting / Misclassification: This is a standard issue when retrospectively analyzing data. There may be additional causes of death worth accounting for. There are also many co-morbidities that impact heart diseases and strokes that may be the underlying issue and cause of death.

  • Database Download Issue: When downloading data from the CDC WONDER database, it alerts that counts may be suppressed if n < 10.
